🏆 1st Runner-Up at the Maze-Solving Robot Competition! 🥈🤖

Navigating an unknown maze autonomously sounds simple until you face strict hardware limits: no wheel encoders and no global mapping.

Securing 2nd Place (1st Runner-Up) with a run time of 33.81 seconds in the Department of Electrical Engineering's Special Term 1 competition was a true test of low-level control, logic design, and rapid iteration.

The Real Problem: Open-Loop Blind Spots
Our initial approach relied on simple time-delay functions for turning. That quickly broke down: across repeated maneuvers, wheel slip, battery drain, and surface friction accumulated severe angular drift, throwing the bot off course.

The Solution: Closed-Loop Relative Distance Referencing
Without encoders to measure turn angle, we engineered two sensor-driven feedback mechanisms:

Wall-Bounded Turns: Before turning near a dead-end wall, the bot stores the front wall distance. As it rotates, the side sensor sweeping toward that wall actively checks for that baseline reading to terminate the turn precisely at 90°.

Corridor Branch Turns (Left-Hand Rule): At open intersections, the bot samples the open corridor width on its left before initiating the turn. During rotation, the front sensor searches for that target distance profile to lock heading alignment.

Handling Diagonal Blindspots (Two Stage Turn Execution):
A pure step and check method sometimes triggered premature stops on angled wall geometries. To eliminate false triggers, we split every turn into two phases:

Phase 1 (Blind Pivot): A fast ~45° rotation to bypass false diagonal reflections.

Phase 2 (Stepped Verification): Micro-stepped rotation with real-time sensor polling until the saved target value is matched.

Combined with a custom lightweight chassis modeled in Blender and 3D printed for structural stability, this control loop made our runs exceptionally repeatable.

Huge congratulations to my teammates Isuru Anjana, Kaveesha Abesekara, and nethmi indima for the relentless debugging, and sincere thanks to the Department of Electrical Engineering faculty and mentors for organizing such an engaging challenge!

It started with a dream: "What if I could just talk to my laptop, and it got things done?" 🎙️

I built it in my free time during my university vacation. Now it's a real-time voice assistant on my own laptop, called Claude ("Hey Claude").

What it does:
→ Controls my desktop, sets reminders and types by voice
→ Explains anything on my screen, searches Google and writes cited research reports
→ Turns my app ideas into prompts for my coding agent
→ Listens first, and stops talking the moment I interrupt
→ Starts with Windows, waits quietly in the background, and pauses the mic with one click.

How it works, for free:
Simple commands run with no AI at all. Everyday chat uses a local model on my laptop. Only harder tasks go to free cloud models, with automatic fallbacks. On battery it switches to the laptop's NPU.

How the logic works:
🎧 Mic → voice detection with a live noise floor
📝 Whisper transcribes on my GPU in about 0.3 seconds
🧠 A smart router decides who answers:
• Simple commands → no AI at all, done in about 0.4 s
• Everyday chat → local Qwen model on my laptop
• Harder tasks → free cloud models (Gemini, then Groq, Mistral and OpenRouter as fallbacks)
🔊 The reply streams back as speech, sentence by sentence

Privacy and safety:
🔒 Most processing stays local
🔒 Risky actions need my face and a spoken "yes", and every approval is logged
⚠️ Still improving: free quotas, short-word recognition, Windows only

My daily routines are faster now: a sentence instead of ten clicks.

From a dream to something I use every day. 🚀
